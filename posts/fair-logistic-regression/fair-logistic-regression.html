Teaching a Robot Banker to Play Fair

What happens when a computer learns from a biased past, and how a single rule can nudge it back

A friendly tour of fairness-constrained logistic regression: why hiding the sensitive attribute isn’t enough, and how to measure the price of fairness.

Imagine a bank that wants to save time. Instead of a person reading every loan application, it wants a computer program to read them and say “yes” or “no”. To teach the program, the bank hands over a big pile of old decisions made by its human loan officers and says: learn to do what they did.

That sounds sensible. But there is a catch. What if the old decisions were unfair?

A world with a thumb on the scale

In this example we build a small make-believe world so we can see exactly what is going on. There are applicants from two groups, which we’ll call Group A and Group B. About three in ten applicants belong to Group B.

Every applicant has a few “skill” scores, things like income stability or repayment history. Here is the important part: the skills are spread out in exactly the same way in both groups. Nobody in Group B is any less capable, on average, than anyone in Group A.

In a fair world, whether you get a loan depends only on those skills. But the historical decisions the bank kept came from a world with a thumb on the scale. Every Group B applicant had a fixed amount knocked off their chances, just for being in Group B.

So the pile of old decisions, the one we’re asking the computer to learn from, quietly contains a bias.

“Just don’t tell it the group!”

The first idea most people have is simple: don’t show the computer which group each applicant is in. If it can’t see the group, surely it can’t discriminate?

Sadly, it isn’t that easy. In our world, each applicant also has two extra details, a bit like a postcode, that happen to differ a little between the groups. They say nothing about whether someone will repay a loan. They are what statisticians call proxies: clues that let you guess the group without being told.

A computer trained to copy the old decisions is very good at spotting patterns. It notices that applicants with a certain kind of “postcode” were turned down more often, and it learns to lean on that clue. Sure enough, when we train a plain model, it puts a negative weight on those postcode-like features and approves Group B applicants far less often than Group A.

NoteThe lesson

Hiding a sensitive attribute is not the same as being fair. If other information can stand in for it, a model can rebuild the bias on its own.

How the robot banker thinks

The model we use is called logistic regression, and it is one of the oldest and most trusted tools in machine learning. You can think of it as a points system:

  • Each feature (each skill score, each postcode detail) earns or loses the applicant some points.
  • The points are added up into a single score.
  • If the score is above zero, the answer is “yes”. Below zero, “no”.

“Learning” just means adjusting how many points each feature is worth until the model’s answers match the training data as closely as possible. Finding the best point values is an optimisation problem: a search for the settings that make some measure of error as small as it can be.

Adding a fairness rule

To push back against the bias, we give the optimiser an extra rule to obey while it learns. The rule we want is demographic parity: both groups should be approved at the same rate.

There is a small snag. Approval rates jump in steps: one applicant flips from “no” to “yes” and the rate suddenly hops. Optimisers prefer smooth hills to staircases. So, following a well-known approach by Zafar and colleagues (2017), we use a smoother stand-in for the rule:

The model’s average score shouldn’t depend on which group you’re in.

We also add a dial, which we’ll call the fairness bound. Turn the dial all the way up and there’s no rule at all: you get the plain, biased model. Turn it all the way down to zero and the two groups must have exactly the same average score. Anywhere in between is a compromise.

What we found

We trained two models: one with no fairness rule, and one with the dial at zero.

The fair model closes most of the gap in approval rates between the two groups. And something interesting happens to its accuracy:

  • Measured against the biased historical decisions, the fair model looks worse. Of course it does: it is refusing to copy the bias.
  • Measured against the fair decisions (the ones that depend only on skill), the fair model looks better.

In real life we would never get to see those “fair decisions”. We only know them here because we built the world ourselves. But it shows something important: if the bias lives in the labels rather than in the people, then “accuracy against the past” can be a misleading report card.

The whole trade-off in one go

One fair model and one unfair model only tell part of the story. What about all the settings in between?

We picked 40 different positions for the fairness dial and asked Jaddle, the optimisation library this example is built with, to train all 40 models at the same time, as one batch. That gives us a full curve showing how accuracy changes as the model becomes fairer, rather than a couple of dots.

Reading the curve from the unfair end to the fair end, accuracy against the biased labels slides down while accuracy against the fair labels climbs up.

The price of fairness

Here is my favourite part. When an optimiser obeys a rule, it can also tell you how much that rule is costing. Mathematicians call this a dual variable or shadow price. Think of it as a receipt that comes with each answer, saying: “if you tightened this rule by one more notch, the training error would go up by this much.”

For our fairness rule, that receipt is a price of fairness. We found that:

  • When the dial is loose enough that the rule isn’t actually stopping anything, the price is zero. The rule is just along for the ride.
  • As the dial tightens, the price climbs steadily. Each extra step towards equal treatment costs a little more than the one before.

That’s useful information for real decision-makers. Rather than arguing in the abstract about whether fairness is “worth it”, you can see the actual exchange rate and talk about it openly.

A few footnotes

Fairness is a deep and much-debated subject, and this example is a small, tidy illustration rather than a final answer.

  • Demographic parity is only one definition of fairness. Others ask for equal error rates, or equal treatment of similar individuals. They can disagree with each other, and choosing between them is a question about values, not just maths.
  • Our world is made up. Real data is messier, and real bias is rarely a single fixed penalty.
  • The rule we used is a stand-in. Making average scores equal tends to bring approval rates together, but it doesn’t guarantee they match exactly.

The takeaway

A model trained to imitate the past will happily imitate the past’s mistakes, even when you hide the obvious clues. Adding a fairness rule to the optimisation is a direct, transparent way to push back. And because the optimiser hands you a price for every rule it follows, you can see exactly what that fairness is costing, and decide with your eyes open.
